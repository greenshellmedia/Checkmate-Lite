Improvement Proof: How to Tell Whether Training Is Changing Your Decisions

One clean game after training feels encouraging, but it is not proof that a habit has changed. Improvement Proof compares a baseline with later relevant opportunities and reports only what the evidence supports.

Published 10 October 2026

Begin with a baseline

A baseline describes the behaviour before focused practice. It needs a specific decision and enough eligible opportunities. “I blunder too much” is too broad. “I move an attacked piece without checking my own forcing moves” can be observed.

Practise the decision you want to change

Use positions that contain the same trigger. Rehearse the process, not only the correct move. For example: notice the attack, list forcing moves for both sides, then compare candidate moves.

Wait for relevant opportunities

Later games must test the same habit

A game with no comparable decision tells us nothing about that objective. Evidence accumulates only when the trained situation genuinely appears again.

One success is encouraging, not conclusive

You may choose correctly once because the position is easier, the move is forced or the pattern is fresh in memory. Several later opportunities provide a fairer view.

Illustrative evidence states

What an honest report can say

Not enough evidence: no later eligible opportunity. Early signal: one or two correct decisions. Mixed: the new process appears, but the old habit still returns. Sustained: enough later opportunities show a consistent change under the stated rule.

Keep rating separate from the habit

A corrected decision may not immediately change results or rating. Opponent strength, openings and many other decisions affect each game. Improvement Proof does not guarantee a rating outcome.

What to do next

Write one measurable decision rule and define what counts as an eligible opportunity. Read the full Improvement Proof explanation and how it fits into personalised weekly coaching.
