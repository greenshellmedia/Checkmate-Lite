Decision DNA: Finding the Choices You Repeat in Chess

Decision DNA is a way of looking across your own games for choices that keep returning. It is less interested in one opening name or one accuracy score than in how you respond when similar decisions arise.

Published 10 October 2026

What Decision DNA looks for

The same position rarely appears twice. The same type of decision appears often. You may exchange tension whenever calculation becomes uncomfortable, push flank pawns before securing the centre, or choose passive defence when active counterplay is available.

Situations need context

A queen trade is not cautious by definition. Trading queens while behind may reduce your chances, while the same trade with an extra pawn may be sensible. Decision DNA needs the situation, the alternatives and the practical result of the choice.

Frequency is not the whole story

A common choice can be healthy. A rare mistake can be severe. Useful analysis considers how often the opportunity occurred, what you chose and whether the choice helped or harmed the position.

From theme to trainable question

Broad labels such as “too aggressive” are difficult to practise. A narrower question is better: when the opponent attacks one of your pieces, do you respond to the attack before checking your own forcing moves?

Illustrative methodology

Releasing central tension

Across six eligible middlegames, a player captures in the centre at the first opportunity. In four of them, keeping the tension would restrict the opponent. The useful pattern is not “captures are bad”. It is “resolving tension before identifying which side benefits”.

What CheckmateMore can and cannot conclude

CheckmateMore can organise evidence from analysed games and surface recurring choices when enough comparable opportunities exist. It should not claim that a small sample defines a player permanently. A pattern is a prompt for attention, not a personality verdict.
