How to Review a Chess Game Without Chasing Accuracy

Accuracy is a useful summary, but it cannot tell you what you misunderstood. A good review starts with your own recollection of the game, then uses the engine to test the decisions that mattered.

Published 10 October 2026

Accuracy is the end of a calculation, not the start of an explanation

An accuracy score compresses a whole game into one number. Two games with the same score can contain very different lessons. In one, you may have played sensibly until a single tactical oversight. In another, you may have made several small positional concessions that were difficult to punish.

The number is useful for comparing the general cleanliness of games. It is poor at answering the question an improving player actually has: what should I notice next time?

Replay the game before opening the engine

Recover what you were trying to do

Play through the moves and stop where you remember thinking for a long time, feeling uncertain or choosing between plans. Write down the candidate moves you considered. If you cannot remember them, note what you understood about the position instead.

Mark decisions, not only mistakes

A move can be objectively fine and still reveal a weak decision process. Suppose you play 18.Rd1 because the rook belongs on an open file, but you never check that Black has 18...Bxh2+. The move may survive tactically in this position, yet the missing safety check is worth recording.

Use the engine to test your account of the game

Now compare your candidates with the engine. Look first at large evaluation changes, missed forcing moves and moments where your plan differed from the best continuation. Do not copy a five-move line without asking why its first move works.

Illustrative example

A quiet move before the blunder

White eventually loses a rook to a fork on move 24. The useful review point may be move 21, when White pushed a pawn and removed the knight's retreat square. The later fork is the visible error. The earlier decision explains why it became possible.

What to do next

Choose one critical position. Record what you considered, what you missed and one short decision rule for a similar position. Then use a game review with clear explanations or try the same method in CheckmateMore Lite.

What Your Repeated Chess Mistakes Are Trying to Tell You

One bad move may be noise. The same kind of decision appearing in several games is a better training signal. The work is to describe the habit precisely enough that you can recognise it over the board.

From a Missed Tactic to a Useful Training Position

A missed tactic is useful training material because it happened in a position you understood imperfectly. The aim is not to memorise one engine line. It is to recognise the trigger when a related position appears again.
