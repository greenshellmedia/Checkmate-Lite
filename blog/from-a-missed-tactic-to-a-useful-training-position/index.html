From a Missed Tactic to a Useful Training Position

A missed tactic is useful training material because it happened in a position you understood imperfectly. The aim is not to memorise one engine line. It is to recognise the trigger when a related position appears again.

Published 10 October 2026

Start with the decision before the tactic

The final combination may be forced, but the learning point often appears one move earlier. What changed? Did a defender move, did a line open, or did the king lose an escape square?

Keep the original context

Record the opening, your plan and the clock situation. A fork missed with thirty seconds left may need a different response from the same fork missed after an eight-minute think.

Build a position that tests recognition

Begin from the last moment when the critical idea was available. Hide the answer. Before moving, list checks, captures and threats, then identify loose pieces and overloaded defenders.

Illustrative training position

Remove the defender first

You missed a knight fork because a pawn defended the entry square. The useful exercise begins before the forcing exchange that removes that pawn. Solving only the final fork would train the last move, not the preparation that made it possible.

Repeat the pattern without copying the answer

Return to the original position after a delay, then practise a related position with the same tactical mechanism. Say the theme and the opponent's best defence. Correct recall is stronger when you can explain why the move works.

Know when a position is unsuitable

Not every engine swing makes a fair puzzle. Exclude positions with several equally good moves, unclear compensation or a solution that depends on a long, unnatural engine line.

What to do next

Save one clean critical position from your latest game and write the recognition cue beside it. See how personal chess tactics practice works, or start with the evergreen guide on reviewing your games.

The CheckmateMore loop

  1. Analyse your games
  2. Understand decisions
  3. Find repeated patterns
  4. Practise key positions
  5. Review later evidence
